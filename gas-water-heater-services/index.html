Most gas water heaters in Milwaukee, WI work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve shuts off the gas for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Milwaukee Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Milwaukee call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

Warning Signs to Watch For

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They measure thermocouple millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. The result is a repair that targets the real fault and a home that is safer for it. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here are our recommendations:

  • Never store flammable liquids near a gas water heater
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
